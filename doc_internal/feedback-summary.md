

# Arrival Resources – Feedback Summary

This document summarizes feedback gathered so far from organizations and community partners who reviewed or discussed the **Arrival Resources** web app. It is intended as an internal synthesis of recurring themes rather than a record of any one conversation.

## Overall Response

The feedback so far has been broadly positive. Organizations generally see the app as a promising and useful tool, especially for supporting newly arrived immigrants and the staff, volunteers, and community partners who help them navigate services. Several people noted that a resource guide of this kind can be especially valuable in the first years of settlement, when everyday systems and local services are unfamiliar.

At the same time, feedback has also made clear that the usefulness of the project depends not only on having information available, but on how that information is accessed, trusted, interpreted, and updated in real-world support settings.

## Main Themes from Feedback

### 1. Clarify the primary user group and usage scenario

One of the most important questions raised across conversations is: **who is this tool mainly for, and in what setting is it meant to be used?**

Different possibilities have emerged:
- newcomers using it independently
- social workers, volunteers, and community organizations using it as a referral tool
- a hybrid model, where organizations mediate access but newcomers may also use it directly

This question matters because it affects many other design decisions, including language access, interface design, mobile usability, printability, and how much explanatory guidance is needed.

### 2. Think about the relationship between digital and physical forms of the resource

Feedback suggests that the app may be most effective when understood not only as a website, but as part of a broader resource ecology that includes trusted physical spaces and interpersonal support.

Participants suggested that printed materials such as leaflets or handouts could complement the web tool, especially in places like community organizations, libraries, and other service settings where people already go for help. Physical materials could also point people toward the broader website through QR codes or other links.

This theme is closely connected to trust. A digital resource may be more likely to be used when it is introduced through an already trusted organization or embedded in a familiar physical environment.

### 3. Language access and communication remain major barriers

A recurring issue is that the site is currently only in English. If the app is meant to be used directly by newcomers, stronger multilingual support will likely be necessary.

Feedback also highlighted that language is only one part of accessibility. Some newcomers may have limited literacy in English, and some may also have limited literacy in their first language. This means that accessibility may depend not only on translation, but also on visual communication, clear navigation, and reduced reliance on dense text.

### 4. Visual design is a strength and should continue to be emphasized

Some of the most positive feedback focused on the visual and navigational aspects of the app. People found the following especially helpful:
- service tags
- color coding
- search by organization name
- map and list views
- printable list outputs

There was also explicit encouragement to keep the guide as visual as possible, using maps, icons, simple graphics, and clear navigation to support accessibility.

### 5. Local specificity is important

Organizations emphasized that the app becomes more useful when it is locally grounded. Geographically specific, neighborhood-level information is often more practical than broad or generic listings.

This includes not only formal service organizations, but also community-based resources and services that are realistically accessible to low-income populations.

### 6. Trust, relationships, and informal knowledge matter

Feedback repeatedly suggested that referrals are rarely based on information alone. In practice, people often rely on:
- recommendations from family, friends, or community members
- social workers and volunteers who know which organizations are responsive
- personal relationships between organizations and service providers
- prior experiences of trust, reputation, or follow-up

This means that the app may be most useful when it supports or complements existing trust-based referral networks rather than assuming that public information alone is sufficient.

### 7. Standardized tags do not always align with how organizations describe themselves

One important theme from organization-specific feedback is that standardized service tags can be useful for search and comparison, but they do not always match how organizations themselves understand or present their work.

For example, one organization preferred **"emotional healing"** over **"mental health"** because the latter felt too clinical and carried unwanted stigma. This suggests that the taxonomy of service tags may need to remain flexible, and that community-facing language may not always map neatly onto standardized categories.

### 8. The scope of “arrival resources” still needs clarification

Feedback also raised an important conceptual question: **what kinds of places and organizations should count as arrival resources?**

In practice, many resources that matter to newcomers are not necessarily newcomer-specific. These may include:
- libraries
- schools
- community health centers
- food access sites
- housing advocacy groups
- gardens and neighborhood gathering spaces

This raises a productive tension. Expanding the database may make it more reflective of real neighborhood life, but it may also blur the project’s focus unless the inclusion criteria are clearly defined.

### 9. Updating and maintaining the resource is an ongoing challenge

Several conversations indirectly raised the question of stewardship. At the moment, the app includes a feedback form, but most useful feedback so far has come through direct conversations rather than through the form.

This raises practical questions such as:
- How should corrections or suggested edits be gathered efficiently?
- Should organizations eventually be able to suggest edits more directly?
- Who is responsible for ongoing verification and updates?
- How can the app remain accurate without becoming too burdensome to maintain?

## Concrete Suggestions and Corrections Raised So Far

### Content / database corrections
- Remove the **financial literacy** tag from **The Welcome Project**.
- Reconsider the **mental health** tag for **Mutual Aid Eastie**; language such as **emotional healing** may better reflect how the organization describes this work.
- Better reflect some of Mutual Aid Eastie’s activities, including support related to child care licensing, driver’s licenses, and seasonal gardening activities.

### Organizations / places suggested for possible inclusion
- Additional organizations were suggested by multiple conversations, including referrals commonly used by service providers.
- Mutual Aid Eastie specifically suggested possible additions such as **City Life / Vida Urbana**, the **Resource Center at 282 Meridian Street**, **GreenRoots**, **Mario Umana Academy**, **Eastie Farm**, and possibly **Central Presente** .
- One organization also offered to share a curated list of frequently used referral organizations to help strengthen the database.

## Design Implications So Far

Taken together, the feedback suggests several likely directions for further development:

1. **Clarify the app’s core use case**  
   Decide whether the main user is the newcomer, the intermediary, or both.

2. **Strengthen the hybrid physical-digital model**  
   Consider how printed materials, organization-based handouts, or location-specific leaflets could complement the website.

3. **Prioritize multilingual and low-literacy accessibility**  
   Language access should be considered alongside visual clarity and ease of navigation.

4. **Continue refining the tagging system**  
   Service tags need to balance consistency, searchability, and sensitivity to how organizations describe themselves.

5. **Clarify inclusion criteria for the database**  
   Define more clearly what counts as an arrival resource and how broad the scope should be.

6. **Plan for long-term maintenance and governance**  
   Consider what kind of update process, feedback loop, or shared stewardship model would be realistic over time.

## Working Conclusion

So far, feedback suggests that **Arrival Resources** is seen as a valuable and needed idea. The strongest positive response is not only that people like the interface, but that many recognize a real gap for practical, localized, and navigable resource information.

At the same time, the feedback also suggests that the project is not simply about building a better directory. It is about understanding how newcomers actually access help: through language, trust, physical settings, social relationships, organizational mediation, and local knowledge. The next stage of development will likely benefit from treating the app not just as a technical product, but as part of a wider support ecosystem.
